# Harborwick 3PL — warehouse brief

*How I, the owner, would walk you through our site. Everything below is how
we actually run the place — if the manual says something different, the manual
is wrong and this brief is right.*

## Who we are

Harborwick 3PL Ltd. is a small third-party logistics company in Port Sellow,
Alveria. We store and distribute packed consumer goods — food, drugstore
products and household items — for about twenty regular clients. Nothing
fancy, but we live off accuracy: our customers trust us because the stock in
the system is the stock on the rack.

We employ around forty people and run two shifts, Monday to Friday: mornings
06:00-14:00, afternoons 14:00-22:00. In peak season we add Saturday morning
overtime. There is always one shift supervisor in charge of the floor.

## The site

One single-storey hall of 6,000 m2 with 10 m clear height, plus a small
office annex. The fenced yard outside is about 3,500 m2 and holds our 8 dock
doors at 1.2 m dock height. Doors 1-4 are inbound only, doors 5-8 outbound
only — the dispatcher assigns every carrier a door and a time slot, and
drivers wait in the marked waiting area, never in the hall.

Speed limits are simple and we enforce them: 10 km/h inside the building,
15 km/h in the yard, and 5 km/h wherever people and trucks meet — marked
pedestrian zones, crossings, and the dock ramps. Pedestrian walkways are
physically separated by yellow barriers and are 1.2 m wide. Main transport
aisles are 4.0 m wide; the rack storage aisles are 3.0 m.

## Zones A-F

The floor is divided into six zones, and everybody learns them on day one:

- **Zone A — receiving (800 m2).** Inbound docks 1-4, unloading and goods
  inspection. Nothing moves past Zone A until the paperwork is checked.
- **Zone B — bulk storage (2,200 m2).** High-bay pallet racking with 2,400
  pallet positions. Each rack bay carries at most 2,700 kg. Free
  block-stacking elsewhere is limited to 3 pallets high and 4.0 m.
- **Zone C — picking (1,200 m2).** Ground-level pick faces and flow racks
  fed from bulk storage.
- **Zone D — dispatch (900 m2).** Outbound staging in front of docks 5-8.
  Wrapped, labelled pallets only.
- **Zone E — cold zone (300 m2).** Chilled storage held between +2 °C and
  +8 °C, with alarms and a manual temperature verification each shift.
- **Zone F — services and control (600 m2).** Value-added packing,
  quarantine, returns inspection, and a separate ventilated hazmat cage of
  60 m2. Hazmat never sits anywhere else, and never more than 4,000 kg
  at a time.

## Equipment

Our fleet is all electric: 6 counterbalance forklifts rated at 2.5 t for
general work, 4 reach trucks rated at 1.6 t for the high bays, 8 electric
pallet jacks, and 12 hand pallet jacks for small moves. Dispatch runs
2 semi-automatic stretch wrappers, and we have 1 cardboard baler. Every
dock door has its own leveller, and the teams share 20 handheld WMS
scanners. No licence, no truck — only I authorise new forklift operators,
and every truck gets a documented check before its first use each day.

## People

Roughly: me as warehouse manager, 2 shift supervisors, 8 forklift
operators, 6 inbound operators, 10 pickers, 2 stock controllers,
4 returns and packing staff, 1 maintenance technician, 2 dispatchers,
and 2 floaters who cover absence — about 38-40 of us in total.

Who does what: I am accountable for the whole site — operations, safety
and the final sign-offs. A shift supervisor runs each shift: the floor
staff and incident response. Forklift operators drive our forklifts, reach
trucks and pallet jacks. Inbound operators unload, count and inspect goods
in Zone A. Pickers pick customer orders in Zone C against scanner tasks.
Stock controllers own inventory accuracy day to day: cycle counts,
recounts and adjustments. Returns staff inspect returns in Zone F and
propose restock, rework or scrap. The maintenance technician maintains our
trucks, racking and dock equipment and logs every repair. Dispatchers book
transport, assign dock doors and release outbound loads.

Responsibilities are fixed: I sign off the stocktake results and any
damaged-goods write-off above 500 EUR; the shift supervisor approves
write-offs up to 500 EUR, the final disposition of inspected returns, and
reviews every incident report. The dispatcher owns dock scheduling.
Only I approve overtime.

## Transport and loads

A pallet, load included, may weigh at most 1,000 kg — that is the number every
capacity on site is built on. Our contracted curtain-side trailers carry 24,000
kg on 33 floor positions, so a trailer takes 24 full-weight pallets. The
delivery van carries 1,200 kg on 6 positions: one full-weight pallet, and
anything heavier goes by trailer. We run 4 outbound doors, each handling 3
trailer loads per shift, which makes 288 full-weight pallets per shift the
most we can promise a client. The hazmat cage's 4,000 kg limit means at most
4 full-weight pallets in the cage.

## How goods flow

A truck arrives at its assigned inbound door. First the documents are
checked — before anything is unloaded. Then we unload, count and inspect;
only inspected goods are booked into the system, and only booked goods go
to put-away in Zone B, E or F. Before each picking shift, the pick faces
in Zone C are replenished from bulk.

Pickers work scanner tasks in Zone C. Every picked item is scan-verified
before it is packed, then labelled and moved to Zone D, wrapped and
staged for its outbound door. The dispatcher releases the load, and the
truck is loaded and sealed.

Returns come back through Zone F: we accept them within 14 days of
delivery, inspect every unit, and only then decide restock, rework or
scrap. Damaged goods go to quarantine the same day they are found.

Counts never stop: cycle counts run every working day on a rotating zone
schedule, and once a year we shut down for a full wall-to-wall stocktake
that I personally sign off. No adjustment is ever posted without a second
count. Racking gets a documented safety inspection every month, and we
run an evacuation drill every quarter — roll call at the assembly point
by the north fence happens before anyone re-enters the building.

## Rules we live by

High-visibility vests and class S3 safety footwear are mandatory for
everyone on the warehouse floor, no exceptions. Forklift operators wear
seat belts whenever the truck is moving. Everything runs off fifteen
written procedures, PR-01 to PR-15:

PR-01 Inbound receiving; PR-02 Put-away to storage; PR-03 Replenishment
of pick faces; PR-04 Order picking; PR-05 Packing and labelling; PR-06
Outbound dispatch; PR-07 Returns handling; PR-08 Stocktaking and cycle
counts; PR-09 Damaged-goods handling; PR-10 Forklift and MHE operation;
PR-11 Pedestrian safety in the warehouse; PR-12 Cold-chain handling;
PR-13 Hazmat handling and spill response; PR-14 Emergency evacuation;
PR-15 End-of-shift handover.
